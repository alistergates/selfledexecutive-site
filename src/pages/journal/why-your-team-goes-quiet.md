It's a pattern I hear about from leaders all the time, especially in flatter, faster organisations where everyone is supposed to be working things out together.

You're on a call with a few of your people. There's a project drifting. Someone has been stuck on a problem for two weeks and hasn't said so. You explain where things need to go, then ask, "Is there anything I can explain further? Are you with me on this?" You get a polite, flat yes. Nobody's really in the conversation, and neither are you.

Many leaders are genuinely puzzled by this. They don't see themselves as intimidating. They've said, out loud, that they want people to ask questions. So why won't anyone?

## A part of them is running the meeting

In Internal Family Systems (IFS) terms, what you're talking to in that moment usually isn't the person. It's a protector.

Junior people often carry parts whose whole job is to avoid being caught out. One keeps quiet so nobody discovers they don't understand. One says "yes, got it" because admitting confusion to the boss feels dangerous. Another keeps working on the problem alone for two weeks rather than put a hand up after twenty minutes.

Those parts aren't being difficult. They learned somewhere, at school, in a previous job or at home, that not knowing gets punished. Your seniority, however friendly you are, is exactly what sets them off.

So "Are you with me?" doesn't reach the person. It reaches the protector, and the protector does its job. It says yes.

## Try asking the part instead

The shift is small and it feels odd the first time. Instead of asking for openness, you get curious about whatever's blocking it.

Something like: *"I notice part of you seems a bit hesitant to raise this. I'm curious what it's worried about."*

Or: *"What would you be afraid might happen if you said you were stuck?"*

That lowers the threat, because you're not saying "why aren't you talking to me?" You're showing interest in the hesitation itself. It tells the protector you've seen it and you aren't going to punish it. People often answer that question honestly in a way they'd never answer "any questions?"

Curiosity is the key word. If you ask it as a technique, it'll land as one. If you're actually interested, it changes the room.

## Check your own parts too

There's a second half to this, and it's the one leaders usually skip.

When the conversation goes flat, what happens in you? For many of the leaders I work with, a particular part steps in: the explainer. It fills the silence with context, theory and best practice, and walks everyone through why it matters. It's hoping that if it explains well enough, people will come with it.

That explainer usually has a reason to exist. It might need to feel useful, or to be seen as the one who knows, or it might simply be anxious when the connection drops. But every extra minute of explaining gives the team's protectors more reason to stay quiet. Now there's a lecture as well as a boss.

So when the call goes quiet, check in with yourself first. Is that you leading, or is it the part that needs them to understand?

## What changes

Leaders who start working this way tend to notice two things. Conversations get slower for a week or two, because asking takes longer than telling. Then people start bringing things earlier: the stuck problem on day two rather than day fourteen, and the half-formed idea they'd never have raised before.

Occasionally someone you'd quietly written off turns out to be exactly who you needed. Given room, a junior person who was getting nowhere following the process picks up a stalled piece of work and turns it round in weeks. That doesn't happen while your explainer is running the meeting.

If your team has gone quiet, it isn't usually a team problem. It's a conversation between your parts and theirs. You can only change one side of it, and that turns out to be enough.
