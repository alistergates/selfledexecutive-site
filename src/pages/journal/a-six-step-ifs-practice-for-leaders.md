Leaders often ask me to write this down so they can use it between sessions. Many have sharp analytical minds and want the method, not just the idea. Here it is.

It's based on what Internal Family Systems (IFS) calls the six Fs, a sequence for getting to know a part of you that's taking over. You can use it before a meeting you're dreading, after one that went badly, or in the middle of a day when something's got under your skin.

## Before you start

Find five or ten minutes and somewhere you won't be interrupted. Close the laptop. You don't need to sit cross-legged or breathe in any special way, but a few slow breaths help.

Bring the situation to mind: the board meeting, the conversation with your co-founder, the client who rants. Notice what happens inside you as you think about it.

## The six steps

**1. Find it.** Where do you feel the reaction? A tight chest, a clenched jaw, a racing mind running through all the things you'll say? There's usually a part behind it. Just locate it.

**2. Focus on it.** Turn your attention towards it instead of pushing it away or arguing with it. You're not trying to fix anything yet. You're just noticing it's there.

**3. Flesh it out.** Get to know it a little. If it had a shape or an image, what would it be? Does it have something to say? How old does it seem? Leaders are often surprised to find the part feels about twelve.

**4. Feel toward it.** This is the step people skip. Ask yourself how you feel towards this part. If the answer is irritation ("I wish it would just stop"), fear or contempt, that's another part talking. Ask that one, politely, to step back for a minute. Keep going until you can feel at least a bit curious about the first one. That curiosity is Self.

**5. Befriend it.** Ask the part how it came to have this job, and how long it's been doing it. Let it know you can see how hard it works. Most protectors have been on duty for decades without anyone saying thank you.

**6. Find its fear.** Then ask the question that usually opens everything up: *"What are you afraid would happen if you didn't do this?"* Keep asking gently until you reach the bottom. It often sounds dramatic: I'd be humiliated, I'd be found out, everything would fall apart, they'd get rid of me. Protectors are almost always trying to prevent something they believe would be catastrophic.

## Closing it off

Thank the part. Really. Let it know you understand what it's trying to do. You might then ask whether it would be willing to let you handle the meeting, staying close and keeping an eye on things, but letting you lead. Many will, at least for a while.

Sometimes a part wants a new job. The critic might be willing to become a coach. The anxious one might be happy to be a scout who flags real risks, rather than raising the alarm about everything.

## During and after

During the meeting, check in once or twice: "Who's here right now?" If the part's taken over, just notice it, and remind it you've got this.

Afterwards, take two minutes. Did the part show up? What did it do? How quickly did you come back to yourself? Leaders who keep this up often find the recovery time shrinks from days to hours, then to minutes.

## When it's not enough

This practice works well with protectors. Sometimes, though, a part won't ease off however much you understand it, because it's guarding something older and more painful. That's normal, and it's the point where working with someone trained in IFS helps most. You don't have to go there on your own.

For now, just practise the steps. Like any leadership habit, it feels clunky for a few weeks and then becomes something you do without thinking.
