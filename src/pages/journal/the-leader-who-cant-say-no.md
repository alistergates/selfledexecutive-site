From the outside you'd never pick it. You're articulate, well liked and good with clients. People describe you as easy to work with. You've been promoted, or you've built something real.

Inside, it's a different story. There's the underperformer you've been meaning to deal with for eight months. There's the board member whose pet project you said yes to, though you thought it was a bad idea. There's the co-founder conversation you rehearse in the shower and never have. And there's the steady, low-grade resentment of a person who keeps agreeing to things they don't want.

Over the years I've worked with a lot of men stuck in what Dr Robert Glover calls the Nice Guy pattern, and it turns up in boardrooms far more than people realise. It isn't only men, either. Anyone who learnt early that being agreeable kept them safe can carry it straight into the corner office.

## Why it survives success

You'd think success would cure people-pleasing. It usually feeds it. The ability to read a room, anticipate what people want and smooth over tension is a real leadership asset, and it's probably helped you get where you are.

The trouble starts when it isn't a skill you choose to use but a part that runs you. In Internal Family Systems (IFS) terms, it's a protector. It scans every interaction for disapproval and acts fast to head it off. It says yes before you've thought about it. It softens the feedback until there's nothing left of it. It agrees in the meeting and complains afterwards.

Underneath it there's usually something younger. It might be a kid who learnt that conflict at home was dangerous, or who got love for being good rather than for being honest. That part doesn't know you're now the CEO. It still thinks someone's disappointment could cost you everything.

## What it costs

It costs you trust, for one. Your team can tell when feedback has been watered down, and the strong performers notice when the weak ones never face consequences. Some of your best people will leave over it and never tell you why.

It costs you decisions. A leader who can't tolerate people being unhappy will keep looking for an option that pleases everyone, and there rarely is one. So decisions drift, or get made by whoever pushes hardest.

And it costs you personally. The resentment has to go somewhere. Often it comes out sideways, as sarcasm, withdrawal or a short fuse at home with the people who feel safest to be annoyed at.

## Why "just be more assertive" doesn't work

You've probably had this advice. Maybe you've done a course. You know the scripts. The problem is that when the moment arrives, the protector moves faster than the script.

Trying to override it usually makes things worse. Either you swing into a clumsy, over-firm version that confirms all its fears, or it simply wins and you avoid the conversation again.

IFS takes a different route. Instead of fighting the people-pleasing part, we get curious about it. What is it trying to prevent? What does it imagine would happen if you said no to the chair? Usually its fear is very specific and very old, and it has never been taken seriously.

When it has been, it can ease off. Then something else is available: you can be clear and kind at the same time. You can disappoint someone and stay in a good relationship with them. That's not a technique. It's what leading from Self looks like.

## What it looks like on the other side

Leaders who do this work don't become hard. Most stay warm and good with people. What changes is that the warmth isn't a strategy any more.

They have the conversation, say what they think in the meeting rather than after it, and let someone be unhappy with a decision without scrambling to fix it. Their teams find them easier to trust, because the yeses mean something now.

If you're the leader everyone likes and nobody quite knows where they stand with, it might be worth asking which part of you is doing the leading.
