In the 1960s a psychiatrist called Stephen Karpman described something he called the drama triangle. He noticed that in conflict, people tend to fall into one of three roles:

- **The persecutor** blames and criticises: "You did this. You didn't do that."
- **The victim** feels powerless and waits to be rescued, or to be told what to do.
- **The rescuer** jumps in to help, explain and fix, often before anyone asked.

The roles feed each other, and people switch between them in seconds. It was described long before Internal Family Systems, but the two fit together neatly. Each role is played by a protector, and each protector gets something out of it.

## The persecutor at the top

Picture a co-founder who's brilliant, driven and very direct. When something goes wrong he goes straight at the person: "When you sent that message, you didn't think, you didn't check, you..."

Every sentence starts with "you". He may be completely unaware of it, and he may have forgotten all about it by the afternoon. But on the receiving end, "you, you, you" lands as blame. It puts the speaker on a pedestal and the listener in the dock.

In an organisation that's trying to be flat and collaborative, it does something particularly corrosive. It recreates parent and child. Grown professionals go back into defensive, childlike behaviour, because that's what being blamed by a powerful figure does to a nervous system. People start working from fear. And because the person at the top does it, it trickles down.

I'd be blunt with that co-founder if he were my client, though not with blame of my own. That pattern came from somewhere. Usually, early on, it was the way he learned to rise: by being the one who was right. It's not relational, though, and it costs more than it looks.

## The rescuer next to him

Now picture his business partner. He's thoughtful and analytical, and he hates conflict. When the co-founder lays into someone, he becomes the scared kid himself for a moment. Then, when he sees a junior team member struggling afterwards, he steps into the rescuer role: "Don't worry, here's how we'll fix it." When he works with juniors day to day, he explains. He gives the context, the theory and why it matters, again and again.

His rescuer is a protector too. Often it gets something important from the role: the validation of being the one who knows, and a way of staying connected to people by being helpful. The cost is that the people he rescues never quite grow. Each explanation, however kind, tells them they don't need to work it out. And he ends up quietly exhausted, holding everyone up.

## The way off the triangle

Each role has a healthier opposite. The persecutor can become someone who's direct without blaming, who says "here's how this lands for me" and "here's what I need" rather than "you did this". The victim can become someone who solves problems and takes ownership. The rescuer can become a coach.

For the rescuer, the shift sounds simple and feels very hard: ask instead of explain. "How would you handle this?" "What do you think happens next if we do that?" Then let them get to the edge of the mistake, and only catch them if they're really about to fall. It slows things down at first, which is exactly why the rescuer part resists it.

That's where IFS comes in. You can't just decide to stop rescuing. The part doing it is protecting you from something, perhaps the discomfort of someone else struggling, or a fear that you're only valued when you're useful. When you get to know that part and it feels understood, it loosens its grip. Coaching becomes possible because the rescuer no longer needs to run the conversation.

## What you can say to the persecutor

You won't change your co-founder. You can change how you meet him. The most useful thing is usually to tell him, calmly and as an equal, how it lands: "When you go 'you, you, you', I hear a disappointed parent, and so does the team. If you've got something to say to me, say it as one co-founder to another." It isn't an attack. You're refusing the role.

Leadership teams are systems, just as an inner world is. When one person steps off the triangle, everyone else has to find a new position. Often that's the start of the culture you were trying to build in the first place.
