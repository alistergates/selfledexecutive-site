Nobody warns you about this part. For years the exit was the goal. You pictured the day it closed, the relief, the freedom, and the time you'd finally have.

Then it happens. There's a dinner, maybe a speech, a lot of congratulations. And a few weeks later you're standing in the kitchen on a Tuesday morning with nowhere to be, and it doesn't feel like freedom. It feels like being switched off at the wall.

It's more common than people admit, because it sounds ungrateful to say out loud. You got what you wanted. Who complains about that?

## A whole team made redundant overnight

Internal Family Systems (IFS) gives a useful way to understand what's going on. For years, a group of parts ran your life. One was always planning, one was always closing, one was always watching for threats, and one kept you going when you were exhausted. They had clear jobs, and they were very good at them.

The day the deal closed, most of them were made redundant.

They don't take that well. Some keep running anyway, so you find yourself checking your phone for emails that aren't coming, or starting three new ventures in a month. Some go quiet, and you feel flat and unmotivated in a way that worries the people around you. Others reach for whatever used to take the edge off: a drink, a bit more risk than is wise, or work that doesn't matter.

Underneath all of it there's usually a question nobody's asked for years: who am I if I'm not running something?

## Why "enjoy it" isn't helpful

Friends tell you to relax and enjoy it. Your partner is pleased to have you home, and then a bit concerned that you're under their feet and restless. You know you should feel grateful, and that makes it worse, because now there's a part of you criticising you for not feeling good.

What's happening isn't a lack of gratitude. Your identity was organised around a role, and the role has gone. The parts that held that identity together need to find out what their job is now. They won't do that by being told to relax.

## What helps

The first thing is to stop treating it as a problem to solve quickly. The urge to jump straight into the next thing is often the old system trying to get its job back. Sometimes the next thing is right. Often it's the same pattern with a new logo.

The second is to get to know the parts that are struggling. The one that can't stop scanning for problems. The one that feels lost without the title. The one that's angry, because it gave everything to the company and now the company has moved on without it. Each has something to say, and most of them have been on duty for decades without a break.

When those parts are heard, things settle. A different kind of question becomes possible. Not "what do I do next?" but "what do I actually want, now that I don't have to prove anything?" Many founders have never asked that question. They've been too busy surviving and then succeeding.

## The quiet opportunity

An exit doesn't have to be a crisis. It can be the first real chance in decades to put a different part of you in charge. Self, in IFS terms, isn't the founder or the deal-maker. It's who you are underneath the roles.

From there, the next chapter tends to look different. It might be another company, an investing role, a board seat, or something you'd never have considered. The difference is that you choose it rather than being driven into it.

If you're in that strange space after the exit, wondering why it doesn't feel how you expected, you're not doing it wrong. A whole team inside you has just lost its job, and it deserves a proper handover.
