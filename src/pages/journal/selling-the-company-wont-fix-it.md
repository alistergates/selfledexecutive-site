I've heard it from founders many times, and I've said it myself. When we sell, the mortgage pressure goes away. When we sell, the school fees are sorted. When we sell, I'll be home more. When we sell, my partner will finally be happy, and so will I.

The sale becomes the answer to everything. And because it's the answer to everything, it quietly starts to run everything.

## What it does to decisions

When one event has to solve all your problems, your thinking narrows. Every decision gets tested against a single question: does this get us to the exit faster?

It's common to see a founder set a hard deadline to sell, whatever the price, because a family commitment seems to depend on it. Lay it out on paper and the logic is often back to front. They're prepared to give away a large share of the company's value to solve a problem that a much smaller decision could fix: a different arrangement at home, a conversation with family, a change to the working week. Everything leads back to one event, and that thinking closes in on them.

Once the sale stops being the cure for everything, options appear. You could run the company for profit and take a dividend. You could make it more valuable so buyers come to you. You might act on an opportunity that's been sitting in plain sight, one that could be worth more than the business as it stands. None of that is visible while the exit has to rescue your life.

## What it does at home

There's a second cost, and partners feel it first.

"Don't worry, when we sell it'll be fine" works the first time and maybe the second. By the fifth year it doesn't land at all. It starts to sound like a promise that keeps not happening, and every delay becomes evidence. For the founder, it becomes another stick to beat himself with: I still haven't done it, I'm still not delivering, I'm still not enough.

Meanwhile the business is quietly being run to fix problems at home, and the home is being run around the business. Neither works well.

## The parts in the room

In Internal Family Systems (IFS) terms, a few parts are usually involved. There's the provider, who measures his worth by what he brings home. There's the critic, who keeps score of every way he's falling short. Often, underneath both, there's an older belief, sometimes inherited, that he isn't good enough unless the numbers prove it. That belief frequently goes back to childhood, to a parent's disappointment about money or status, picked up long before he had a company of his own.

Those parts turn the exit into salvation. It isn't really a business plan. It's a way to finally feel enough.

When we work with them, and the provider and critic feel understood rather than overruled, the sale can go back to being what it should be: one option among several, pursued on its merits.

## What to do instead

A few things I'd suggest to any founder who recognises this.

**Stop saying "when we sell".** Especially at home. Replace it with what you're actually doing this quarter.

**Separate the problems.** Write down what the sale is meant to fix. Then ask, for each one, whether there's a smaller, faster way to solve it. Often there is: help from family, a change of logistics, a different working pattern.

**Focus on value, not the exit.** A profitable, growing company gets approached. A founder desperate to sell gets squeezed. The part of the market you can't control will do what it does. The value of the business is largely in your hands.

**Do the thing only you can do.** For many founders that means being in front of customers, not managing the anxiety of the exit.

The sale might still come, and it might be the right move. But it can't carry your marriage, your worth and your children's future. Asking it to is how good companies get sold too cheap, and good founders get worn down.
